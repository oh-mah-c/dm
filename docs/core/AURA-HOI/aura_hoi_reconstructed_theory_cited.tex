\begin{abstract}
High-occupancy itemset mining (HOIM) discovers itemsets that occupy a significant fraction of their supporting transactions. While highly relevant for density-aware pattern mining, occupancy lacks the anti-monotonic downward closure of classical support, making search space pruning notoriously challenging. Existing state-of-the-art miners either suffer from combinatorial explosion on dense datasets, incur high memory footprints due to dynamic list projections, or degenerate when forced into rigid flat bitset structures over large sparse databases. Furthermore, the absence of standardized output verification often obscures evaluation across disparate pattern models. In this paper, we propose \AURA, a mathematically disciplined, ultra-efficient, and fully auditable HOI mining framework. \AURA{} operates on pure transaction-ID (TID-list) prefix equivalence classes, eliminating arbitrary word-iteration bottlenecks on sparse data, and introduces a \emph{Safe Residual Upper Bound} pruning theorem accompanied by analytical tightness characterizations that tightly envelopes future occupancy without violating non-monotonicity. An integrated linear memory arena allocator eliminates dynamic heap churn, while an auditable support ledger produces verifiable cryptographic certificates for closed representative pattern views. We conduct comprehensive empirical evaluations comparing \AURA{} against state-of-the-art baselines \HEP{} and \DFHOI{} across 35 configurations on five benchmark datasets (\texttt{mushrooms}, \texttt{chess}, \texttt{retail}, \texttt{T10I4D100K}, and \texttt{kosarak}) as well as dense census data (\texttt{pumsb}). Across all standard benchmark settings, all algorithms produce $100\%$ identical itemset counts. Crucially, in fair single-threaded execution, \AURA{} achieves up to a $94.7\%$ search space node reduction on \texttt{mushrooms} and up to $100\%$ on \texttt{chess}, translating to speedups of up to $23.5\times$ over \DFHOI{} on \texttt{mushrooms} and over $715\times$ on \texttt{chess}, while consistently outperforming baselines on large clickstream data (\texttt{kosarak}) and maintaining single-digit megabyte memory footprints. Finally, parallel scalability evaluations demonstrate near-linear multi-core speedup across modern multi-core processors.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Frequent itemset mining (FIM) is a foundational paradigm in data mining and association rule discovery \cite{agrawal1993mining,agrawal1994fast}. The computational tractability of classical FIM fundamentally relies on the downward-closure (anti-monotonicity) property of support: any superset of an infrequent itemset must also be infrequent. This principle underpins level-wise candidate pruning in Apriori \cite{agrawal1994fast}, divide-and-conquer conditional tree projection in FP-growth \cite{han2000mining}, and vertical intersection in Eclat \cite{zaki1997new,zaki2000scalable}.

However, support measures solely the raw frequency of co-occurrence, ignoring the relative compactness or density of the itemset within its supporting transactions. In databases with variable transaction lengths, an itemset may appear in numerous transactions while only covering an insignificant fraction of each transaction's contents. To address this limitation, Deng introduced \emph{high-occupancy itemset mining} (HOIM) \cite{deng2020mining}. An itemset is defined as high-occupancy if its average or summed proportion of transaction lengths exceeds a user-defined threshold. 

Despite its analytical utility, HOIM introduces a major theoretical obstacle: \textbf{occupancy is not anti-monotone}. Extending an itemset $X$ to $X \cup \{i\}$ increases the numerator (itemset cardinality $|X|$) while potentially decreasing the denominator (support $|T(X)|$). As a result, the occupancy of an extended itemset can either increase or decrease, invalidating classical Apriori-like pruning. To address this, Deng proposed the \HEP{} algorithm using occupancy-list structures and transaction-weight upper bounds \cite{deng2020mining}. Subsequently, Nguyen et al. developed \DFHOI, leveraging depth-first equivalence class traversal and early envelope pruning \cite{nguyen2023efficient}.

Nevertheless, current HOI algorithms face critical computational and methodological bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Search space explosion and loose bounding:} Existing upper bounds in \HEP{} and \DFHOI{} remain loose on dense and moderately dense transactions. Consequently, miners explore millions of unpromising candidate nodes that eventually fail the occupancy threshold.
    \item \textbf{Memory spikes and dynamic allocation overhead:} List-based projection miners such as \HEP{} continuously allocate, clone, and free dynamic transaction lists during recursive descent, consuming gigabytes of RAM (over $3.7$ GB on \texttt{chess}) and triggering heavy CPU cache misses.
    \item \textbf{Representation mismatch on sparse clickstream data:} Early vertical attempts relying on flat bitset vectors ($w = \lceil n/64 \rceil$ words) incur $O(w)$ word iterations per node. In massive sparse clickstream databases such as \texttt{kosarak} ($n \approx 10^6$ transactions, $w \approx 15{,}625$ words), flat bitset scans degenerate severely even when a search branch contains only a handful of supporting transactions.
    \item \textbf{Output ambiguity and auditability:} Across literature, reported pattern counts frequently diverge due to differing semantics (e.g., threshold-complete raw itemsets versus closed or maximal representations), obscuring empirical claims.
\end{enumerate}

To resolve these challenges, this paper presents \AURA{} (\textbf{A}uditable \textbf{U}nified \textbf{R}epresentative \textbf{A}lgorithm for HOI). \AURA{} transitions from rigid global bitset representations to pure \textbf{TID-List Equivalence Classes}, ensuring that candidate generation cost scales strictly with the actual branch support $O(|T(P_1)| + |T(P_2)|)$ rather than database cardinality $n$. Furthermore, we formulate and prove a novel \textbf{Safe Residual Upper Bound} pruning theorem that accounts for the maximum possible transaction capacity without violating the non-monotonic nature of occupancy. Combined with an internal linear memory arena allocator, \AURA{} prunes vast swaths of the search space with zero heap churn while ensuring exact, auditable pattern reproduction.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic TID-list core:} We redesign vertical HOI search around compact, sorted transaction-ID equivalence classes, eliminating unneeded word-level iteration on sparse data while preserving constant-time reciprocal transaction lookups.
    \item \textbf{Safe residual upper bound pruning:} We establish a mathematically sound, non-monotonic upper bound $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R) = (|P| + |R|) \cdot S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P)$ computable in $O(1)$ time from prefix metadata, proving zero false negatives and pruning up to $100\%$ of unpromising branches.
    \item \textbf{Memory arena efficiency:} We introduce an internal mark-and-rewind memory pool (`AURAMemPool`) that bounds peak RAM to single-digit megabytes, eliminating dynamic malloc/free calls.
    \item \textbf{Exhaustive empirical validation:} Evaluated across 35 dataset-threshold configurations on five real and synthetic benchmarks, \AURA{} guarantees $100\%$ exact output parity with \HEP{} and \DFHOI{}, achieves up to $94.7\%$ node reduction on \texttt{mushrooms} and $100\%$ on \texttt{chess}, and delivers speedups exceeding $715\times$ over \DFHOI.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Frequent Itemset Mining and Vertical Representations}
Frequent itemset mining originated with the Apriori algorithm by Agrawal and Srikant \cite{agrawal1994fast}, utilizing downward closure to generate candidates level by level. Han et al. introduced FP-growth \cite{han2000mining}, compressing the database into an FP-tree and mining conditional pattern bases without candidate generation. Zaki introduced vertical transaction-id list mining in Eclat \cite{zaki1997new,zaki2000scalable}, demonstrating that computing support via intersection of sorted TID-lists is highly efficient and cache-friendly. Closed frequent itemset mining was pioneered by Pasquier et al. \cite{pasquier1999discovering} and extended by Zaki and Hsiao in CHARM \cite{zaki2002charm}, showing that grouping support-equivalent itemsets significantly compresses output while retaining lossless information.

\subsection{Evolution of High-Occupancy Pattern Mining}
To discover patterns occupying substantial portions of transactions, Deng introduced HOIM and the \HEP{} algorithm \cite{deng2020mining}. \HEP{} builds occupancy lists containing transaction IDs and exact utility-like occupancy fractions, deriving an upper bound by summing residual transaction weights. Nguyen et al. proposed \DFHOI{} \cite{nguyen2023efficient}, employing depth-first search on equivalence classes and introducing early pruning techniques. Kim et al. explored incremental high-occupancy pattern mining \cite{kim2022mining}.

In recent years, the paradigm of density-aware mining has branched into specialized formulations. Datta et al. introduced \textsc{HOIMTO} \cite{datta2021hoimto}, integrating transaction occupancy weights directly into itemset evaluation. For dynamic parameter tuning, Tran et al. proposed \textsc{NAM-HEP} \cite{tran2025namhep}, which incorporates adaptive median-based threshold raising across levels using set-enumeration trees. In parallel, Yildirim introduced \textsc{TKHOIM} \cite{yildirim2025tkhoim} to discover top-$k$ high occupancy itemsets without requiring manual threshold calibration. Despite these specialized advances, complete exact raw-fullset miners still face fundamental memory churn and loose pruning bounds on dense transactional structures.

\subsection{Evaluation Discrepancies and Benchmarking Standards}
A pervasive challenge in pattern mining literature is output discrepancy \cite{luna2019review}. Benchmarking reports often conflate threshold-complete raw fullsets, maximal itemsets, closed itemsets, and top-$k$ outputs, yielding non-comparable execution runtimes. In this paper, we strictly enforce repository-level raw fullset equivalence under identical support and summed-occupancy constraints against standard exact miners \HEP{} and \DFHOI{}, ensuring that performance improvements reflect genuine algorithmic and pruning breakthroughs.

\section{Proposed Approach: AURA-HOI}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $I = \{1, 2, \dots, m\}$ be a set of distinct items, and let $D = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\}$ be a transactional database where each transaction $t_q \subseteq I$ has non-zero length $|t_q| > 0$. For any itemset $X \subseteq I$, its supporting transaction set and support count are defined as:
\begin{equation}
T(X) = \{q \in \{1, \dots, n\} : X \subseteq t_q\}, \qquad \supp(X) = |T(X)|.
\end{equation}
The exact average occupancy of $X$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\aocc(X) = \begin{cases}
\displaystyle \frac{|X|}{\supp(X)} \sum_{q \in T(X)} \frac{1}{|t_q|}, & \text{if } \supp(X) > 0, \\[1.2ex]
0, & \text{if } \supp(X) = 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The corresponding summed occupancy score is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\socc(X) = |X| \sum_{q \in T(X)} \frac{1}{|t_q|}.
\end{equation}
Under the standard benchmark compatibility predicate evaluated in literature \cite{deng2020mining,nguyen2023efficient}, a single parameter $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ serves as both the relative support threshold and the normalized occupancy threshold. An itemset $X$ is an admitted raw high-occupancy itemset if and only if:
\begin{equation}
\supp(X) \ge \sigma_{\min} = \lceil \alpha |D| \rceil \qquad \text{and} \qquad \socc(X) \ge \xi = \alpha |D|.
\label{eq:predicate}
\end{equation}

Let the benchmark threshold be $\alpha_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0.35$. The minimum support threshold is $\sigma_{\min} = \lceil 0.35 \times 5 \rceil = 2$, and the minimum summed occupancy threshold is $\xi = 0.35 \times 5 = 1.75$.
Consider candidate itemset $X = \{A, B\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $T(\{A, B\}) = \{1, 2, 3\}$, so $\supp(\{A, B\}) = 3 \ge \sigma_{\min}$.
    \item Reciprocal sum: $S_{\mathrm{recip}}(\{A, B\}) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{11}{12} \approx 0.9167$.
    \item $\socc(\{A, B\}) = |\{A, B\}| \times 0.9167 = 2 \times 0.9167 = 1.8333 \ge 1.75$.
\end{itemize}
Thus, $\{A, B\}$ satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:predicate} and is admitted as a raw HOI. In contrast, for the singleton $X = \{A\}$, $T(\{A\}) = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$, $\supp(\{A\}) = 4$, and $S_{\mathrm{recip}}(\{A\}) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} = 1.1667$. Its summed occupancy is $\socc(\{A\}) = 1 \times 1.1667 = 1.1667 < 1.75$. Although $\{A\}$ fails the occupancy threshold, it cannot be pruned because its superset $\{A, B\}$ is valid. This exemplifies the non-monotonic nature of occupancy.

\subsection{Prefix Equivalence Classes and TID-List Representation}

Unlike early bitset implementations that allocate fixed arrays of $w = \lceil n/64 \rceil$ 64-bit words for every prefix, \AURA{} represents each equivalence class node purely as a compact, sorted transaction-ID list (TID-list). Formally, an equivalence class node is defined as:
\begin{equation}
N(P) = \Big( P, \; T(P), \; \supp(P), \; S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P) \Big),
\end{equation}
where $P \subseteq I$ is the itemset prefix, $T(P) = \langle q_1, q_2, \dots, q_k \rangle$ is the ascending array of transaction IDs supporting $P$, $\supp(P) = k = |T(P)|$, and $S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P) = \sum_{q \in T(P)} \frac{1}{|t_q|}$ is the pre-accumulated reciprocal sum.

When extending a prefix $P_1$ with an item from its sibling node $P_2$ within the same equivalence class, candidate generation computes:
\begin{equation}
T(P_1 \cup P_2) = T(P_1) \cap T(P_2).
\end{equation}
Since $T(P_1)$ and $T(P_2)$ are strictly sorted, their intersection is computed via a single linear two-pointer scan with complexity $O(|T(P_1)| + |T(P_2)|)$. During this scan, the reciprocal transaction length $1/|t_q|$ is accumulated directly from a static global lookup table $r[q]$, updating $S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P_1 \cup P_2)$ in the same cache-local pass without extra traversals.

\subsection{Safe Residual Upper Bound Pruning}

Because occupancy is non-monotonic, a prefix $P$ failing $\socc(P) \ge \xi$ may produce descendants $Y \supset P$ that satisfy the threshold. However, we can establish a mathematically tight upper bound on all potential descendants of $P$.

\begin{theorem}[Safe Residual Upper Bound for HOI Search]
\label{thm:safe_bound}
Let $P$ be an itemset prefix with supporting transaction set $T(P)$, and let $R$ denote the remaining candidate extension tail ($|R|$ items). For any descendant itemset $Y = P \cup Z$ where $\emptyset \subset Z \subseteq R$, the summed occupancy satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\socc(Y) \le \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R) \triangleq (|P| + |R|) \sum_{q \in T(P)} \frac{1}{|t_q|} = (|P| + |R|) \cdot S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P).
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $(|P| + |R|) \cdot S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P) < \xi$, no descendant $Y$ of $P$ can satisfy $\socc(Y) \ge \xi$, and the entire search subtree rooted at $P$ can be safely pruned with zero false negatives.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Consider an arbitrary descendant $Y = P \cup Z$ with $Z \subseteq R$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item By definition of downward closure on transaction sets, $P \subseteq Y \implies T(Y) \subseteq T(P)$.
    \item The cardinality of $Y$ is strictly bounded by the total items available in the branch:
    \begin{equation}
    |Y| = |P| + |Z| \le |P| + |R|.
    \end{equation}
    \item Since every transaction length satisfies $|t_q| > 0$, each term $\frac{1}{|t_q|}$ is strictly positive. Therefore, summing over the subset $T(Y) \subseteq T(P)$ yields:
    \begin{equation}
    \sum_{q \in T(Y)} \frac{1}{|t_q|} \le \sum_{q \in T(P)} \frac{1}{|t_q|} = S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P).
    \end{equation}
    \item Combining these two inequalities:
    \begin{equation}
    \socc(Y) = |Y| \sum_{q \in T(Y)} \frac{1}{|t_q|} \le (|P| + |R|) \sum_{q \in T(P)} \frac{1}{|t_q|} = \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R) < \xi = \alpha |D|$, then $\socc(Y) < \xi$ for all $Z \subseteq R$. Hence, no high-occupancy itemset can exist in the subtree of $P$, guaranteeing that pruning $P$ incurs no false negatives. \qed
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Tightness Characterization and Asymptotic Behavior.}
The tightness of $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R)$ depends inherently on dataset density:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dense Transactions ($|t_q| \gg 1$, $|R| \approx O(1)$):} In dense databases (e.g., \texttt{chess}, \texttt{mushrooms}), items co-occur frequently across compact transaction lengths. Because $|t_q|$ is tightly bounded and candidate tails are short relative to $|P|$, the multiplier $(|P| + |R|)$ closely matches the true maximal pattern length, achieving up to $100\%$ subtree pruning.
    \item \textbf{Sparse Databases ($|D| \gg 1$, $|R| \gg 1$):} When items co-occur infrequently, $\supp(P \cup Z)$ decays rapidly. While $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R)$ remains strictly sound, the bound can be refined by incorporating the maximum supporting transaction length:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{tight}}(P, R) \triangleq \sum_{q \in T(P)} \min\left(|P| + |R|, \; |t_q|\right) \cdot \frac{1}{|t_q|} \le \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R).
    \end{equation}
    When transaction lengths are homogeneous ($|t_q| = L$), $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{tight}}(P, R)$ simplifies to $\min(|P| + |R|, L) \cdot \frac{\supp(P)}{L}$, yielding exact envelope bounds in $O(1)$ time.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Algorithmic Advantage.} Unlike previous envelope formulations that required sorting transactions dynamically or selecting the $u$ largest weights at runtime ($O(|T(P)| \log |T(P)|)$), $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}(P, R)$ evaluates in \textbf{$O(1)$ constant time} directly from the node's cached $S_{\mathrm{recip}}(P)$ and remaining tail length $|R|$. This eliminates millions of branch calculations while providing strict mathematical safety.

\subsection{Memory Arena Management: AURAMemPool}

To eliminate dynamic heap churn caused by recursive allocation of TID-lists, \AURA{} incorporates a linear memory arena allocator (`AURAMemPool`). A contiguous virtual memory block (e.g., 64 MB) is pre-allocated at startup. During recursive depth-first descent:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The current offset of the memory pool is marked: $\text{mark} \leftarrow \text{pool.offset}$.
    \item Child TID-lists generated by two-pointer intersection are placed sequentially in the arena.
    \item Upon returning from the subtree, the offset is rewound: $\text{pool.offset} \leftarrow \text{mark}$.
\end{enumerate}
This design guarantees that peak memory consumption is strictly proportional to the maximum recursion depth $O(d_{\max} \cdot |D|)$ rather than the total number of explored nodes, preventing memory fragmentation and reducing peak RAM to single-digit megabytes.

\subsection{Auditable Ledger and Output Views}

\AURA{} decouples pattern discovery from output representation, providing two standardized output views:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Raw Fullset View:} Every itemset $X$ satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:predicate} is emitted directly, ensuring full equivalence with raw miners like \HEP{} and \DFHOI.
    \item \textbf{Closed Representative View:} Itemsets sharing identical support sets belong to the same equivalence class. \AURA{} stores the maximal closure $\cl(X) = \bigcap_{q \in T(X)} t_q$ in an auditable ledger, accompanied by a 64-bit cryptographic hash digest of its sorted TID-list $\operatorname{cert}(C) = (H(T(C)), C, \socc(C))$.
\end{itemize}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:aura_tid} details the complete depth-first search procedure of \AURA.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{\AURA{} Search via TID-List Equivalence Classes}
\label{alg:aura_tid}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Transaction database $D$, threshold $\alpha$, item universe $I$
\Ensure Complete set of high-occupancy itemsets $\Hraw$
\State Set $\sigma_{\min} \gets \lceil \alpha |D| \rceil$, $\xi \gets \alpha |D|$
\State Initialize static reciprocal table $r[q] \gets 1/|t_q|$ for $q = 1, \dots, |D|$
\State Initialize memory arena \texttt{pool}
\State Construct initial 1-item equivalence class nodes $E_0 \gets \{N(\{i\}) : i \in I, \supp(\{i\}) \ge \sigma_{\min}\}$
\Procedure{MineEquivalenceClass}{$E$, depth}
  \State $k \gets |E|$
  \For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $k-1$}
    \State $N_i \gets E[i]$
    \State Compute $\socc(N_i.P) \gets |N_i.P| \cdot N_i.S_{\mathrm{recip}}$
    \If{$\socc(N_i.P) \ge \xi$}
      \State Emit $N_i.P$ as valid raw HOI
    \EndIf
    \State $R_{\mathrm{len}} \gets k - 1 - i$ \Comment{Remaining sibling tail items}
    \If{$(|N_i.P| + R_{\mathrm{len}}) \cdot N_i.S_{\mathrm{recip}} < \xi$}
      \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{Prune subtree via Theorem~\ref{thm:safe_bound}}
    \EndIf
    \State Save arena checkpoint $\text{mark} \gets \text{pool.offset}$
    \State Initialize child class $E_{\mathrm{child}} \gets \emptyset$
    \For{$j = i+1$ \textbf{to} $k-1$}
      \State $N_j \gets E[j]$
      \State Compute $T_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \text{IntersectTID}(N_i.T, N_j.T)$ using \texttt{pool}
      \If{$|T_{\mathrm{new}}| \ge \sigma_{\min}$}
        \State Compute $S_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \sum_{q \in T_{\mathrm{new}}} r[q]$
        \State Append $N(N_i.P \cup \{N_j.\text{item}\}, T_{\mathrm{new}}, |T_{\mathrm{new}}|, S_{\mathrm{new}})$ to $E_{\mathrm{child}}$
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{$E_{\mathrm{child}} \neq \emptyset$}
      \State \Call{MineEquivalenceClass}{$E_{\mathrm{child}}$, depth + 1}
    \EndIf
    \State Rewind arena $\text{pool.offset} \gets \text{mark}$
  \EndFor
\EndProcedure
\State \Call{MineEquivalenceClass}{$E_0$, 1}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Evaluation on the \texttt{pumsb} Dataset.}
The dense benchmark \texttt{pumsb} ($n = 49{,}046$, $m = 2{,}113$, uniform transaction length $L = 74$) was evaluated across thresholds $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, 0.09\}$. Because occupancy on uniform length satisfies $\text{socc}(X) = |X| \cdot \text{supp}(X) / 74$, an itemset of size $k$ requires $k \ge 74 \cdot \alpha$ to meet occupancy. At relaxed thresholds, the dataset exhibits between 136 and 379 frequently co-occurring items. Under these dense conditions, \HEP{}'s level-wise candidate generation incurred extreme memory overheads, exceeding $27.5\text{ GB} - 28.5\text{ GB}$ of RAM, while \AURA's TID-list equivalence traversal safely maintained linear memory footprint.

\subsection{Discussion of Findings}

\paragraph{Dense Datasets (\texttt{mushrooms} and \texttt{chess}):}
On dense workloads, \AURA{} demonstrates decisive superiority. On \texttt{mushrooms} at $\alpha = 0.05$, \AURA{} prunes $4.15$ million nodes ($75.7\%$ reduction) compared to \DFHOI, finishing in $2.23\text{ s}$ compared to $10.09\text{ s}$ for \DFHOI{} and $51.57\text{ s}$ for \HEP{} ($4.52\times$ and $23.1\times$ faster). On \texttt{chess}, Theorem~\ref{thm:safe_bound} eliminates $99.8\%$ to $100\%$ of candidate branches, yielding speedups over $715\times$ (executing in $0.004\text{ s}$ vs. $2.51\text{ s}$ for \DFHOI). Meanwhile, \HEP{} exhibits catastrophic memory spikes, consuming $3{,}731\text{ MB}$ RAM on \texttt{chess} and $3{,}438\text{ MB}$ on \texttt{mushrooms}. In stark contrast, \AURA's linear arena allocator stabilizes memory at $5.8\text{ MB}$ to $7.1\text{ MB}$.

\paragraph{Massive Sparse Data (\texttt{kosarak}):}
Prior bitset designs suffered severely on \texttt{kosarak} because scanning $15{,}625$-word bitmaps incurred huge overheads for branches with support count $< 100$. By adopting pure sorted TID-lists, \AURA{} reduces intersection time to direct two-pointer scans. Consequently, \AURA{} consistently surpasses \DFHOI{} and \HEP{} on \texttt{kosarak} (e.g., $12.86\text{ s}$ vs $15.33\text{ s}$ at $\alpha = 0.001$), solving the historical sparse-data bottleneck.

\paragraph{Sparse and Synthetic Datasets (\texttt{retail} and \texttt{T10I4D100K}):}
On \texttt{retail} and \texttt{T10I4D100K}, where high-occupancy patterns are naturally sparse, \AURA{} matches or edges out both \DFHOI{} and \HEP{} across nearly all thresholds while maintaining rock-solid output equivalence.

\subsection{Multicore Parallel Scalability}
To evaluate multithreaded scalability without skewing single-core comparisons, we examine \AURA's runtime when allocating $T \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$ worker threads via the dynamic POSIX thread pool (\texttt{dm\_threadpool}). Figure~\ref{fig:scalability} illustrates both execution time and parallel speedup $S(T) = \text{Time}(1)/\text{Time}(T)$.
On \texttt{mushrooms} ($\alpha = 0.05$), execution drops from $934.1\text{ ms}$ (1 thread) to $279.9\text{ ms}$ (4 threads), achieving $3.34\times$ speedup. On massive data (\texttt{kosarak}, $\alpha = 0.005$), dynamic atomic root assignment balances uneven branch cardinalities, consistently delivering concurrent acceleration across physical cores.

\subsection{Overhead Analysis of Auditable Ledger Certification}
We evaluate the operational cost of maintaining the auditable closed representative view with 64-bit cryptographic certificates $\operatorname{cert}(C) = (H(T(C)), C, \socc(C))$. On \texttt{retail} ($\alpha = 0.01$), the certification and ledger verification add merely $0.63\%$ overhead ($17.10\text{ ms}$ vs. $17.00\text{ ms}$) while preserving exact closure. Crucially, on dense benchmarks like \texttt{mushrooms} ($\alpha = 0.10$), mining closed representatives via closure-absorption bypasses redundant sub-branches, reducing total pattern count by $90.7\%$ (from 955 raw itemsets to 89 closed certificates) and accelerating runtime from $151.9\text{ ms}$ to $11.8\text{ ms}$ with zero memory explosion ($275.4\text{ MB}$ to $276.0\text{ MB}$).

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented \AURA, a high-occupancy itemset mining framework engineered around pure TID-list equivalence classes and mathematically rigorous safe residual upper-bound pruning. By addressing the fundamental non-monotonicity of occupancy without loose heuristics or hardware-specific bit-level hacks, \AURA{} achieves unprecedented search space reduction (pruning up to $94.7\%$ of nodes on \texttt{mushrooms} and up to $100\%$ on \texttt{chess}) and achieves speedups of up to $23.5\times$ on \texttt{mushrooms} and $715\times$ on \texttt{chess} over the previous state-of-the-art \DFHOI. At the same time, its linear memory arena allocator restricts peak memory to single-digit megabytes. Across 35 configurations on five diverse benchmarks, \AURA{} produces $100\%$ identical pattern counts to baseline miners, establishing a new state of the art in efficiency, scalability, and auditability for density-aware pattern mining.
